*** Probability and statistics
**** Probability theory
Here, we talk a lot about *probability measures $P$* on some space $\Omega$.
These are functions on *sets*, so that 
- $P(\Omega) = 1$
- $P(A) \in [0,1]$ for any $A \subset \Omega$
- $P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B)$ if $A \cap B = \emptyset$.
*** Notes on probability.                                            :B_note:
:PROPERTIES:
:BEAMER_env: note
:END:

**** Measures: Functions on sets.

The idea of probability is that it is a type of measure. Measures in everyday life include  length, mass, or area. The bigger the 'area' of a set, the more likely it is. It also has the same properties as areas, meaning that the total area of two separate sets is their joint area.

**** Subsets of $\Omega$
When $\Omega$ is a finite set, we can consider any subset $A \subset \Omega$. However, in general we are restricted to an \emph{algebra} of subsets. However, this is just a technical issue.


*** Statistics
In statistics, we are interested in random variables $x, y, \ldots$
which are *functions*  $x : \Omega \to X$, $y : \Omega \to Y$ etc.

- We write $\Pr(x)$ as a shorthand for /the probability distribution of $x$/.
- The distribution of $x$ arises in the following way:
  1. Sample $\omega$ from $P$
  2. Calculate $x(\omega)$.
- We can get a *probability measure $P_x$* by measuring how often $x$ falls in various sets $A \subset X$:
\[
P_x(A) \defn P(\{\omega : x(\omega) \in A\})
\]
Here, $\{\omega : x(\omega) \in A\}$ is literally the set of random outcomes $\omega$ for which $x(\omega)$ is in the set $A$. To avoid writing this cumbersome expression, we simply write
$\Pr(x)$ as a general shorthand. This allows us to also write
\[
\Pr(x \in A) = P_x(A).
\]
*** Notes on random variables.                                       :B_note:
:PROPERTIES:
:BEAMER_env: note
:END:
The previous slide shows how we can get probability measures $P_x$ for random variables $x$. It sometimes is necessary to think about the actual measure $P_x$, but we usually do not have to assign probabilities to sets! We can just say $\Pr(x)$ loosely, without specifying whether or not we are talking about the probability of a set, or even just the related density.
*** Joint distributions, multiple variables
It is still possible to define a probability measure for multiple variables. Typically, it is done as follows.
- Let $x : \omega \to X$, $y: \omega \to Y$ be two RVs.
- The measure $P_{x,y}$ has to be defined on the subsets of $X \times Y$.
\[
P_{x,y}(A) = P(\{\omega : (x(\omega), y(\omega)) \in A\}
\]
However, we usually just speak of the joint distribution $\Pr(x,y)$. If necessary, we may say $\Pr((x,y) \in A)$ for specific probabilities.

**** Factoring joint distributions.
For any sequence of random variables $x_1, \ldots, x_T$, their joint distribution can be written in terms of the factors
\begin{align*}
\Pr(x_1, \ldots, x_T)
&= \Pr(x_1)\Pr(x_2 | x_1) \Pr(x_3 | x_2, x_1) \cdots \Pr(x_T | x_1, \ldots, x_{T-1})\\
&= \Pr(x_1) \prod_{t=2}^T \Pr(x_t | x_1, \ldots, x_{t-1}) 
\end{align*}
However, these factors can be simplified if there only few dependencies between the random variables. The structure of dependencies can be formalised in terms of graphical models.

*** Graphical models and factorisation
- Graphical models tell us what directly depends on what
- They allow us to simplify the *joint distribution* of the variables
- This is formed into a *product of factors*, one for each variable.
**** Example
    \begin{tikzpicture}

      \node[RV] at (0,0) (xB) {$x_1$};
      \node[RV] at (1,1) (xD) {$x_2$};
      \draw[->] (xB)--(xD);
      \draw[->] (xD)--(xi);

    This graphical model implies the factorisation 
    \[
    \Pr(x_1, x_2, x_3) = 
    \Pr(x_3 \mid x_2, x_1) 
    \Pr(x_2 \mid x_1) 
    \Pr(x_1) = 
\Pr(x_3 \mid x_2) \Pr(x_2 \mid x_1) \Pr(x_1)
    \]
     Notice that for each factor $\Pr(x_i \mid x_j)$, $x_j$ is the *parent* of $x_i$.

** Time-series
*** Time-series

A sequence of data $x_1, \ldots, x_t$, is called a *time series*. Examples include
- Text
- Speech
- Music
- Prices
- Weather

Generally, in a time series the future observations only depend on past ones. The general idea is captured by *Markov* models. Even though they appear simple, they are general enough to encompass all possible models for time series.

**** The Markov property      
In a Markov chain, the observations form a chain. In general, the following independence condition holds:
\[
\Pr(x_{t+1} \mid x_t, x_{t-1}, \ldots, x_1) = \Pr(x_{t+1} \mid x_t)
\]
meaning that knowing $x_t$ is sufficient to predict $x_{t+1}$, and the previous observations are not required.
*** Hidden Markov model

**** Hidden states
In a hidden Markov model, the observations $y_t$ are not a chain. The variable $x_t$ is not seen, and called a *hidden state*. The Markov property still holds for $x_t$, 
\[
\Pr(x_{t+1} \mid x_t, x_{t-1}, \ldots, x_1) = \Pr(x_{t+1} \mid x_t)
\]
but not for $y_t$. However, if we know the current state, it is sufficient for us to predict the observations:
\[
\Pr(y_t \mid y_{t-1}, \ldots, y_1, x_t, x_{t-1}, \ldots, x_1) = \Pr(y_t \mid x_t).
\]

*** Conditional independence (general)
- Consider variables $x_1, \ldots, x_n$.
- Let $B, D$ be subsets of $[n]$, and
- $\bx_B \defn (x_i)_{i \in B}$ be the variables with indices in $B$.
- $\bx_{-j} \defn (x_i)_{i \neq j}$ all the variables apart from $x_j$.
**** Conditional independence :B_definition:
     :PROPERTIES:
     :BEAMER_env: definition
     :END:
  We say $x_i$ is *conditionally independent* of $\bx_B$ given $\bx_D$ and write 
  \[x_i \indep \bx_B \mid \bx_D\]
  if and only if:
  \[
  \Pr(x_i, \bx_B \mid \bx_D)
  =
  \Pr(x_i \mid \bx_D)
  \Pr(\bx_B \mid \bx_D).
  \]
- For this to hold in graphical model, $D$ must separate $i$ from $B$ in the graph.

** Classification: Discriminative modelling
*** Discriminative modelling
**** General idea
- Data $(x_t,y_t)$.
- Need to model $\Pr(y | x)$.
- No need to model $\Pr(x | y), \Pr(y)$.
**** Examples
- Logistic regression.
- k-Nearest Neighbour
- Multi-Layer Perceptron
*** What we model.                                                   :B_note:
:PROPERTIES:
:BEAMER_env: note
:END:
Of course, if we want to model the distribution $\Pr(x,y)$ and $x, y$ are not independent, then it should not matter whether or not we factorise $\Pr(x | y) \Pr(y)$ or $\Pr(x) \Pr(y | x)$.

However, in order for us to perform classification or regression, we only need $\Pr(y|x)$. Then there is no need to model $\Pr(x)$ explicitly. 

** Classification: Generative modelling
*** Generative modelling
**** General idea
- Data $(x_t,y_t)$.
- Need to model $\Pr(y | x)$.
- Model the *complete* data distribution: $\Pr(x | y)$, $\Pr(x)$, $\Pr(y)$.
- Calculate \(  \Pr(y | x) = \frac{\Pr(x | y) \Pr(y)}{\Pr(x)}. \)
**** Examples
- *Naive Bayes* classifier.
- *Gaussian mixture* model.
**** Modelling the data distribution in classification
- Need to estimate the density $\Pr(x | y)$ for each class $y$.
- Need to estimate $\Pr(y)$.

A *Markov model* obeys
\[
\Pr_\vparam(x_{k+1} | x_k, \ldots, x_1) = \Pr_\vparam(x_{k+1} | x_k)
\]
i.e. the graphical model is a chain. We are usually interested in *homogeneous* models, where
\[
\Pr_\vparam(x_{k+1} = i \mid x_k = j) = \param_{i,j} \qquad \forall k
\]
**** Inference for finite Markov models
Finite Markov models are special cases of discrete Bayesian networks: we can consider each random variable $x_{t+1}$ as having a Multinomial distribution, whose parameter depends on what the value of $x_t$.
So, when $x_t = i$, then $x_{t+1}$ is multinomial with parameter $\vparam_i$. 

*** Inference for the Multinomial distribution
**** The Multinomial distribution
If $x_t \mid \vparam \sim \Mult(\vparam)$ then $\Pr(x_t = i \mid \vparam) = \param_i$. 

**** The Dirichlet distribution
The conjugate prior for a Multinomial parameter is the Dirichlet distribution, which is the multivariate extension of the Beta. Its density is $p(\vparam \mid \vectorsym{\alpha}) \propto \prod_{i=1}^n \param_i^{\alpha_i - 1}$.

**** The Dirichlet-Multinomial conjugate pair
- If $x_t \in [n]$ then $x_{t+1} \mid \vparam, x_t = i \sim \Mult(\vparam_i)$, $\vparam_i \in \Simplex^n$
- Prior $\vparam_i \mid \vectorsym{\alpha} \sim \Dir(\vectorsym{\alpha})$ for all $i \in [n]$.
- Posterior $\vparam_i \mid x_1, \ldots, x_t, \vectorsym{\alpha} \sim \Dir(\vectorsym{\alpha}^{(t)})$ with
  \[
  \alpha^{t}_{i,j} = \alpha_{i,j} + \sum_{k=1}^t \ind{x_k = i \wedge x_{k+1} = j},
  \qquad
  \vectorsym{\alpha}^0 =   \vectorsym{\alpha}.
  \]
As you can see, the parameters of the Dirichlet distribution are updated the same way as those of the beta: simply increase the counts of the observed outcomes.

** Gradient algorithms
*** Gradient ascent
In the following we use $\param$ for *all* the parameters of the Gaussian mixture model,
with $x = (x_1, \ldots, x_T)$ and $z = (z_1, \ldots, z_T)$
**** Objective function
One way to estimate $\param$ is through maximising the likelihood
$L(\param) = \Pr(x | \param)$
**** Marginalisation over latent variable
However, we need to marginalise over all values $z$
\[
L(\param) = \sum_z \Pr(z, x | \param)
\]
For $T$ data points and $k$ different values of $z_t$, there are $k^T$ vectors $z$ to sum over.
**** Gradient ascent
If we can calculate the gradient of $L$, we can use gradient ascent to update our parameters:
\[
\param^{(n+1)} = \param^{(n)} + \alpha \nabla_\param L(\param).
\]
*** Gradient calculation (method I)
Here we use the *log trick*: $\nabla \ln f(\param) = \nabla f(\param) / f(\param)$.
\begin{align}
\nabla_\param L(\param)
& = \sum_z \nabla_\param \Pr(z, x \mid \param) 
\\
&= \sum_z  \Pr(z, x \mid \param) \nabla_\param \ln \Pr(z, x \mid \param)
\\
&= \sum_z  \Pr(x \mid z, \param)\Pr(z \mid \param) \nabla_\param \ln \Pr(z, x \mid \param)
\end{align}

We can approximate the sum by sampling $z^{(i)}$ to obtain:\footnote{Not really necessary for GMMs, but needed for other latent variable models.}
\[
\frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^m \Pr(x \mid z^{(i)}, \param) \nabla_\param \ln \Pr(z^{(i)}, x \mid \param),
\qquad z^{(i)} \sim \Pr(z  \mid \param)
\]

We can use either the exact gradient or the stochastic estimate to perform (stochastic) gradient ascent.

*** Gradient calculation (method II)
The problem with the previous method is that we sample from the prior $\Pr(z \mid \param)$. Let us now start with the log-likelihood and use the log trick *twice*
\begin{align}
\nabla_\param \ln \Pr(x \mid \param)
& = \frac{\nabla \Pr(x \mid \param)}{\Pr(x \mid \param)}\\
&= \frac{\sum_z \nabla \Pr(x, z \mid \param)}{\Pr(x \mid \param)}\\
&= \frac{\sum_z \Pr(x, z \mid \param) \nabla \ln \Pr(x, z \mid \param) }{\Pr(x \mid \param)}\\
&= \sum_z \Pr(z \mid x, \param) \nabla \ln \Pr(x, z \mid \param).
\end{align}
- Methods I, II have the same stationary points.
- However, since method I ascends the likelihood, and method II the log-likelihood, performance is better with method II for large $T$, as method I's gradient is vanishingly small. 
- Method II gives you lower-variance Monte-Carlo estimates if you sample (for more complex models), because you use $\Pr(z \mid x, \param)$, which is more informative, though harder for complex models.

** Expectation maximisation

*** A lower bound on the likelihood
# SUGGEST: label the inequality step (line 903) "by the Gibbs inequality", and say the
# notes slide that follows states it. "Not negative" (line 911) holds because $z$ is
# discrete, so $\mathbb{H} \geq 0$.
For any distribution $G(z)$, and specifically for
$G(z) = \Pr(z | x, \param^{(k)})$:
\begin{align*}
\ln \Pr(x | \alert{\param})
& = \sum_z G(z) \ln \Pr(x | \alert{\param})
 = \sum_z G(z) \ln [\Pr(x, z | \alert{\param}) / \Pr(z | x, \alert{\param})]
\\
& = \sum_z G(z) [\ln \Pr(x, z | \alert{\param}) - \ln \Pr(z | x, \alert{\param})]
\\
& = \sum_z G(z) \ln \Pr(x, z | \alert{\param}) - \sum_z G(z) \ln \Pr(z | x, \alert{\param})
\\
& = \sum_z \Pr(z | x, \param^{(k)}) \ln \Pr(x, z | \alert{\param}) - \sum_z \Pr(z | x, \param^{(k)}) \ln \Pr(z | x, \alert{\param})
\\
& \geq \sum_z \Pr(z | x, \param^{(k)}) \ln \Pr(x, z | \alert{\param}) - \sum_z \Pr(z | x, \param^{(k)}) \ln \Pr(z | x, \param^{(k)})
\\
& = Q(\alert{\param} \mid \param^{(k)}) +\mathbb{H}(z \mid x, \param^{(k)}),
\end{align*}
where 
\[
\mathbb{H}(z \mid  x, \param^{(k)}) = - \sum_z  \Pr(z \mid  x, \param^{(k)}) \ln \Pr(z \mid x, \param^{(k)})
\]
is the entropy of $z$ for a fixed $x, \param^{(k)}$. As this is not negative, $\ln \Pr(x | \param) \geq Q(\param \mid \param^{(k)})$.
*** Some information theory.                                         :B_note:

Information theory notation can be a bit confusing. Sometimes we talk about random variables $\omega$, and sometimes about probability measures $P$. This is context-dependent.
**** Entropy
For a random variable $\omega$ under distribution $P$, we denote the entropy as
\[
 \mathbb{H}_P(\omega) \equiv \mathbb{H}(P) \equiv \mathbb{H}(\omega) = - \sum_{\omega \in \Omega} P(\omega) \ln P(\omega).
\]
**** KL Divergence
For two probabilities $P, Q$ over random outcomes in the same space $\Omega$, we define
\[
D_{KL}(P \|Q) = \sum_{\omega \in \Omega} P(\omega) \ln \frac{P(\omega)}{Q(\omega)}
\]
**** The Gibbs Inequality
$D_{KL}(P \|Q)  \geq 0$, or $\sum_x \ln P(x) P(x) \geq \sum_x \ln Q(x) P(x)$.
