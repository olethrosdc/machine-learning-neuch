*** The General problem
- Observations $D$.
- Nuisance variables $z$.
- Unknown parameter $\param$.
- Direct calculation of any of these terms can be infeasible:
\[
\bel(\param \mid D) = \frac{P_\param(D) \bel(\param)}{\sum_{\param'} P_{\param'}(D) \bel(\param')},
\qquad
P_\param(D) = \sum_z P_\param(D, z).
\]
*** Common methods
- Monte Carlo 
- Variational Bayes
- Approximate Bayesian Computation (ABC)
- Stochastic Variational Inference

* Monte-Carlo Methods
** The Monte-Carlo sampler
To approximate an expectation, we can approximate the integral
\[
\E_P(f) \defn \int_\Omega f(\omega) dP(\omega)
\]
by sampling $\omega$ from $P$ and taking the sample mean:
\[
\E_P(f) \approx \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=1}^N f(\omega^{(n)}),
\]
which as we already know, is an unbiased estimator.

We can also approximate the probabilities of events $A \subset \Omega$:
\begin{align}
P(A) &= \int_A dP(\omega) = \int_\Omega \ind{\omega \in A} dP(\omega)\\
&= \E_P[\ind{\omega \in A}]
\approx \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=1}^N \ind{\omega^{(n)} \in A}.
\end{align}
But what if we can not sample from $P$?

*** Sampling

- In the data analysis setting, the samples come from *nature's unknown distribution $P$*.  Here, however we are using sampling to perform *approximate computation*. Nevertheless, the mathetmatical tools are the same.
- Because we define the sampling, we can decide what distribution to sample from. But how do we sample something random in the first place?
- If we have a fair coin, we can get $n$ random bits, to obtain a number in $\{0, 1, \ldots, 2^{n}-1\}$. Dividing by $2^n$, we can approxiamte the uniform distribution in $[0,1]$.
- In practice, we only have a few random bits, which we combine with deterministic algorithms (Pseudo-Random Number Generators) to get the illusion of randomness.
- Alternative notions of randomnes include Kolmogoroff Complexity.

If we cannot sample from $P$, but can sample from some other distribution $Q$, we can use this trick\footnote{Statisticians hate it!}. Let's write it first out for finite $\Omega$, for simplicity
\[
\E_P(f) \defn \sum_{\omega \in \Omega} f(\omega) \frac{P(\omega)}{Q(\omega)} Q(\omega)
= \E_Q(f P / Q) 
\]
Now this is the expectation of $f(\omega)P(\omega)/Q(\omega)$ with respect to $Q$. So, if we can sample from $Q$ instead, we can still approximate the integral for the general case.
\[
\E_P(f) \defn \int_\Omega f(\omega) \frac{dP(\omega)}{dQ(\omega)} dQ(\omega)
\approx \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=1}^N f(\omega^{(n)}) \frac{dP(\omega^{(n)})}{dQ(\omega^{(n)})},
\qquad \omega^{(n)} \sim Q.
\]
It is easy to show that this estimator is also unbiased. However, as the probability ratio can be very large, it has a very high variance.

So, how *can* we sample from $P$ for arbitrary distributions?

** Basic sampling theory
*** Inversion sampler
$F(u) \defn \Pr(x \leq u) = P(\{\omega : x(\omega) \leq u\})$ is the CDF of $x$.
- Sample $u$ uniformly in $[0,1]$
- Set $x = F^{-1}(u)$, i.e. $v: \Pr(x \leq v) = u$

*** Rejection Sampler
- Input: $\epsilon > 0$, distribution $Q$ with $\epsilon P \leq Q$.
- Repeat:
- $\hat{x} \sim Q$.
- $u \sim \Unif[0,1]$
- Until $u \leq \epsilon P(\hat{x}) / Q(\hat{x})$.
- Return $\hat{x}$
*** Notes
- Useful for sampling from a known distribution $P$.
- A variant of rejection is used for ABC (see later).

Approximating a density is tricky, so we will instead approximate the posterior probability that the parameter is in $B \subset \Param$:
\[
\bel(B \mid D) 
= \int_{B} d\bel(\param | D)
=\frac{\int_{B} P_\param(D) d \bel(\param)}{\int_{\Param} P_{\param'}(D) d\bel(\param')}
=\frac{\int_{B} P_\param(D) d \bel(\param)}{\int_{\Param} P_{\param'}(D) d\bel(\param')}
\]
We can approximate the integrals by sampling from the prior $\bel$:
\[
\int_{B} P_\param(D) d \bel(\param)
\approx
\frac{1}{N}
\sum_{n=1}^N \ind{\pn \in B} P_{\pn}(D),
\qquad \pn \sim \bel.
\]
- Sampling from the prior is *inefficient*: almost all the samples land where the likelihood is *near 0*.
- The estimator has high bias and variance, because the denominator is *also* an estimate.
- So, we can use *Markov Chain* Monte Carlo. This lets us sample a
  sequence $\pn$ which \alert{converges asymptotically}\footnote{You don't have to worry about asymptotics in this course.} to $\bel(\pn |
  D)$.

** Metropolis-Hastings I
*** Algorithm (symmetric version)
- Input: Proposal distribution $Q(x | x') = Q(x' | x)$
- At time $n$:
- $\hat{x} \sim Q(x | x^{(n)})$
- w.p. $P(\hat{x}) / P(x^{(n)})$, $x^{(n+1)} = \hat{x}$ else $x^{(n+1)} = x^{(n)}$ (capped at 1)
*** Application to posterior sampling:
The denominator cancels out, leading to:
\[
\frac{\bel(\param' \mid D)}{\bel(\param \mid D)}
= 
\frac{P_{\param'}(D) \bel(\param')}{P_{\param}(D) \bel(\param)}
\]
The only question is which proposal to use.
** Metropolis-Hastings II
*** Algorithm
- Input: Proposal distribution $Q(x | x')$, likelihood $P$ (up to constant factor)
- At time $n$:
- $\hat{x} | x^{(n)} \sim Q(x | x^{(n)})$
- With probability (capped at 1)
  \[
  \frac{P(\hat{x}) Q( x^{(n)} | \hat{x} )}{P(x^{(n)}) Q(\hat{x} | x^{(n)})},
  \]
  set $x^{(n+1)} = \hat{x}$
- Otherwise $x^{(n+1)} = x^{(n)}$
*** Application to posterior sampling:
The $\Pr_\bel(D)$ term cancels out, leading to:
\[
\frac{\bel(\param' \mid D) Q(\param \mid \param') }{\bel(\param \mid D)  Q(\param' \mid \param)}
= 
\frac{P_{\param'}(D) \bel(\param')  Q(\param \mid \param')}{P_{\param}(D) \bel(\param)  Q(\param' \mid \param)}
\]

*** Stationary distribution
The Markov chain defined by the M-H algorithm must have a unique stationary distribution
\[
\sigma = \sigma \vectorsym{P},
\]
where $\vectorsym{P}$ is the transition kernel of the chain with
\[
P_{ij} = \Pr(x^{(n+1)} = j \mid x^{(n)} = i).
\]
In addition, $\lim_{n \to \infty} \vectorsym{P}^k = \vectorsym{1} \sigma$.
*** Sufficient conditions
- If the transition kernel satisfies \alert{detailed balance}:
\[
P(x' | x) \sigma(x) = P(x | x') \sigma(x')
\]
then $\sigma(x)$ is a stationary distribution.
- If the Markov chain is \alert{ergodic} then there is a unique $\sigma$.

In standard probabilistic learning, we compare the probability $P_\param(D)$ of the data $D$ under different parameters $\param$.
ABC is used when $P_\param(D)$ is unavailable. There are two settings:
 
*** Simulator: we can sample from $P_\param(D)$.
- An example is a weather model. We can generate data (predictions) from the simulator for various parameters $\param$.
- Here $\param$ includes e.g. how much light the clouds disperse, or
heat the ocean can store. 
- There is no analytic likelihood defined: we randomise by starting the weather model with different random seeds.
*** Intractable likelihood: hard to calculate $P_\param(D)$.
There may be a way to calculate the probability, but it is too costly.

*** General idea
Replace probabilities with a *distance* between *statistics*

** ABC Rejection sampling
*** A metric $\rho$ over datasets
- $\rho(D, D')$ is distance between datasets.
- We can use that to define a rejection sampler
*** ABC Rejection Sampling
- \textbf{Input}: $\epsilon > 0$.
- Sample $\param' \sim \bel(\param)$
- Sample $D' \sim P_{\param'}$.
- If $\rho(D, D') \leq \epsilon$, accept $\param'$
*** Theorem
If $\rho(D, D') = \|f(D) - f(D')\|$ and $f$ is a \alert{sufficient statistic} and $\epsilon = 0$ then ABC Rejection Sampling is exact.

** ABC: a toy example
*** Data
- $D = (x_1, \ldots, x_n)$
- $x_i \mid \param \sim \Normal(\param, 1)$
- prior $\param \sim \Normal(0, 10)$. (we don't know what $\param$ is.)
*** ABC parameters
-  Statistic $f(D)$ = sample mean (sufficient here);
  \[
  \rho(D, D') = |f(D) - f(D')|
  \]
- Repeat: sample $\param'$ from the prior, simulate $D'$, accept if $\rho(D, D') \leq \epsilon$.

** Which method when?
 - Expectation under $P$, and you can sample from $P$: plain Monte Carlo.
 - You can evaluate $P$ but not sample from it, and $Q$ is close to $P$: importance or rejection sampling.
 - Posterior far from the prior, or many latent variables: MCMC (Metropolis-Hastings in general; Gibbs when the conditionals are tractable).
 - Likelihood intractable but you can simulate data: ABC.
 - Variational inference: not mentioned. Uses analytical approximations of the posterior. Fast but can be wrong.
# - Stochastic Gradient ascent: (seen in GMM lecture) basically a Monte Carlo gradient method, used when we have a lot of data.
# - Expectation Maximisation: (seen in GMM lecture) ok for small-medium data

*** Parameter estimation target
- Samples from the posterior: MC, MC-IS, MCMC, ABC.
- Approximate posterior: VI, SVI.
- Point estimate: EM, stochastic gradient (maximum likelihood or MAP).
[All methods give some distribution over the other latent variables]
  
** Combinations
If some of these methods involve an optimisation step, you can mix them with Gradient Ascent (GA), stochastic GA (SGA), Expectation Maximisation (EM). Classical approaches are:

- EM with an approximate posterior = variational EM
- SGA + VI = SVI
- GA + MCMC = HMC; SGA + MCMC = SGLD / SGHMC
